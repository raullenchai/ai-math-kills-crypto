8 selected result families · 10 manuscripts · Source snapshot: October 7, 2026

This analysis examines the path from a mathematical advance to someone stealing BTC/ETH keys, forging validator signatures, or defeating KZG and the ZK systems that depend on it.

The pinned October 7 source snapshot contains 722 manuscripts across 372 result families. The live repository may change after this snapshot. We selected 8 candidate families with plausible cryptographic connections and examined 10 manuscripts in more detail. This was a focused review, not an exhaustive audit of the entire collection.

Our conclusion: these reviewed results do not establish a practical, end-to-end attack-cost reduction against the blockchain targets we examined.

Some of the claimed theoretical advances are substantial. But the missing steps between those advances and a usable attack are substantial too. Here is what we found, assuming the stated theorems hold.

1) #142 — deterministic polynomial factoring over prime fields

This factors a polynomial over a known prime field. It does not factor an RSA modulus.

Randomized expected-polynomial-time algorithms for the polynomial task already existed. The new result removes randomness; it does not establish a speed advantage. A usable new reduction from RSA factoring or elliptic-curve key recovery to this task is still missing.

We cannot turn this theorem into “RSA-2048 is now cheaper to break.”

A follow-up question concerns the tools inside #142: Jacobian division and norm-equation methods might inspire other algorithms. Their stated settings involve restricted curve structure and divisibility promises. The paper’s discrete-log subroutine handles small-prime components; it is not a generic large-prime discrete-log solver. No effective reduction from these tools to extracting a deployed curve’s hidden scalar, with practical target-sized costs, is established here. This is a research direction, not a demonstrated attack.

2) #279 — exact quantum integer factoring

The ideal quantum circuit succeeds with probability exactly 1 over a fixed finite gate set, with polynomial worst-case resource bounds.

Shor already provided a quantum factoring route; candidates can be checked and success amplified through repetition. The new exactness guarantee is a theoretical improvement, but the paper establishes no lower practical qubit, gate-count or runtime requirement.

It also does not directly supply a new elliptic-curve discrete-log attack. The practical hardware gap remains.

3) #103 — L = RL = BPL

For the polynomial-time deterministic simulations compared here, the workspace bound improves from O(log² n) to O(log n). That is a major theoretical space improvement.

But the theorem needs an appropriate randomized log-space program as its input. It does not provide the program that solves secp256k1 key recovery. Finding such a target solver would itself already be a cryptanalytic breakthrough.

Less workspace for a simulation does not give us a new key-recovery work estimate.

4) #107 — faster dense matrix multiplication

This is the candidate with the most direct-looking connection to an existing attack component: linear algebra in NFS/TNFS attacks on finite-field discrete logarithms.

The field matters. The reported bounds are ω ≤ 2.25 over the complex numbers; ω < 2.258 outside an uncomputed finite exceptional-characteristic set; and ω < 2.371054886006746 over every fixed field.

Cryptanalytic relation matrices are sparse. Common solvers exploit that sparsity, with a rough cost around w·N² field operations, where w is row weight. A better dense-multiplication exponent does not by itself establish a faster sparse solve or a cheaper complete attack.

The striking complex-field bound cannot simply be inserted into a BLS/KZG security estimate.

5) #109 — integer multiplication below n log n

The reported asymptotic bound is O(n(lg n)^(1−κ)), with κ = 2^−182, in a fixed multitape Turing-machine model.

The paper describes extremely large constants and thresholds, and uses schoolbook multiplication below its cutoff. It establishes no speed gain at cryptographic sizes.

Substituting 256 or 2048 into the asymptotic expression would not establish an implementation speedup.

6) #029 — the proof input behind #142

The relevant connection is its uniform Hecke zero-free theorem, not the primitive-root count itself. It supports #142’s proof chain. It does not supply an independent RSA or curve-key recovery algorithm.

7) #002 — an exact BSD formula under stated conditions

This concerns global arithmetic of elliptic curves. No effective algorithm is supplied for extracting the hidden scalar d from a finite-field public key Q = dG.

8) #030 — modularity over imaginary quadratic fields

The structural theorem becomes more general. But the natural j=0 CM lifts of the blockchain curves discussed already had classical modularity results. A usable bridge from the new theorem to target discrete logs remains missing.

How big is the demonstrated security drop?

For secp256k1, roughly 2^128 group operations remains a representative generic classical attack scale, with existing constant-factor improvements. This is not a security lower bound.

These results supply no established new end-to-end figure such as 2^100 or 2^80. For BLS/KZG, the cost also depends on the curve, target instance and precomputation model; there is no single honest number covering every system.

We also separated illustrative function values from attack costs. For example, changing c from 2.371177 to 2.25 makes the normalized function m^c about 5.33× smaller at m = 10^6. That is not a measured speedup, 81% fewer attack operations, or a loss of 2.415 security bits.

The actual consequences, if the missing bridges were built, would differ:

• secp256k1 key recovery → ECDSA/Schnorr forgery → control of assets whose public keys and spending conditions permit it.
• Relevant BLS key recovery → forged validator messages; consensus impact depends on stake and protocol conditions.
• Relevant KZG trapdoor recovery → false openings; matching ZK systems and applications need separate analysis.
• RSA factoring → RSA-dependent systems; it is not the native BTC/ETH signature route.

That is what the diagrams show: reported results, existing algorithms, missing attack bridges, practical resource gaps, and conditional on-chain consequences.

Cryptanalytic progress can be nonlinear.

A reusable mathematical method could resolve several missing bridges at once. A practical attack on an affected discrete-log group could spread across the schemes using that group. In finite-field DLP attacks, substantial precomputation can be reused across targets in the same field; each target still requires further work. And recovering one KZG setup trapdoor can undermine many commitments using that setup, without recovering a fresh trapdoor for each commitment.

Ethereum’s KZG commitments and validator BLS signatures both use BLS12-381. A practical discrete-log algorithm applicable to the relevant groups could undermine both systems. By contrast, leaking one KZG setup’s τ defeats binding for commitments under that SRS without itself recovering validator keys. A solver for a relevant pairing target field would not automatically attack secp256k1. Consensus impact depends on the effective validator stake and messages an attacker can forge, not simply on recovering one BLS key.

Those are real amplification mechanisms. Our dependency diagrams are not a timeline: the number of missing arrows is not a measure of remaining research effort. The review does not establish that these eight results have triggered such a cascade, or that future progress will be slow or incremental.

Conclusion: the public evidence in these eight candidate families does not establish that practical blockchain key theft or proof forgery has become substantially cheaper.

This is an assessment of demonstrated attacks. It does not establish future safety, a probability of a break, or a percentage of “progress toward breaking crypto.”

The underlying source-statement and arithmetic analysis went through three review rounds with Claude Opus 5.5, with the displayed arithmetic separately recomputed. The nonlinear scenarios above are conditional extrapolations, not demonstrated attacks. We did not verify the original mathematical proofs; model agreement is not a correctness certificate.

Why can’t a+b+c be used directly to measure the size of an advance?

First break the same attack into its computational steps

W_total = W_reduction + W_subroutine + W_secret-extraction + W_other-stages

These W must be converted to comparable units of work. If an effective reduction already exists, one of the subroutines can be replaced and the sum recomputed. If the reduction does not exist, the other terms are not known constants, and it cannot be assumed that only a few small engineering steps remain.

In sequential execution, peak memory is usually the maximum over the stages, counting data retained at the same time; in parallel execution, concurrent storage and coordination overhead must also be added. Hence M cannot be added directly to W or T.

A large speedup of a substep may also affect the whole only slightly

T_new / T_old ≈ (1−f) + f/s

Assuming the same route and the same hardware, if a step that originally took 10% of the time is sped up 100×, the whole is sped up only about 1.11×; if it originally took 90%, the whole is sped up about 9.17×.

This is a hypothetical example illustrating how bottlenecks propagate, not a measured figure from the papers above. When only memory is reduced, a speedup cannot be derived automatically from this formula; it may improve deployability, or it may trade more time for space.

The accurate conclusion for #103: The space-saving aspect is a major theoretical advance; there is no new conclusion on the work or actual time of a complete break, because the target BPL solver has not been given. The gap here is the core algorithm, not “most of the break is done and only deployment remains”.

Final impact: what could break, and what cannot be derived directly?

Account signatures → asset control

Actually recovering a secp256k1 private key allows forging that key’s ECDSA / Schnorr signatures. EOA takeover or spending BTC assets further requires that the corresponding public key be obtainable, that the assets still be spendable, and that the other conditions of the script or wallet be satisfied.

BLS → authenticity of consensus messages

Actually recovering validator signing keys, or achieving a valid forgery, can threaten message authenticity. Whether finality/safety is affected depends on the stake weight that can be forged and on the specific protocol conditions.

KZG → commitment binding and related proofs

Recovering the corresponding SRS trapdoor, or constructing a valid false opening, may break blob commitment binding and the verification that depends on it.EIP-4844 uses KZG; concrete consensus or asset consequences further require an upper-layer exploitation path.

ZK / RSA → judge each specific system individually

ZK is a class of protocols, not a single cryptographic assumption; pairing/KZG attacks cannot be generalized to all ZK. RSA private-key recovery affects services and protocols that use RSA and cannot be directly recast as recovery of commonly used curve account keys.

Attack gaps: 0 / 3 thresholds No target-attack gain over existing randomized factoring is shown for #142

#142: factoring polynomials in 𝔽ₚ[x]

The input is a known prime p and an explicitly given polynomial; the novelty is determinism.

→

Turn computing d from a secp256k1 public key Q=dG into a polynomial factoring problem of controllable size. None exists at present.

→
Gap 2: practical complexity

For a 256-bit target, compute d within available hardware and time; the theoretical upper bound of #142 is far from enough.

→
Bad outcome: forged account authorization

Whoever holds d can sign for ETH EOAs / BTC ECDSA or Taproot Schnorr outputs with exposed public keys and move the spendable assets.

“Polynomial” shares its name with KZG polynomial commitments, but the tasks are different.

→

Path D · #279 → RSA (side branch, not the main BTC/ETH signature)

#279: exact quantum integer factoring

Fixed finite gate set, ideal circuit, success probability 1; claims no practical resource reduction.

→
Gap 1: a large enough fault-tolerant quantum machine

The logical gates, error correction and runtime required to reach target RSA parameters.

→
Gap 2: real-hardware demonstration

Successfully and reproducibly factor a key of real security size.

→
Bad outcome: RSA keys fail

Directly affects RSA-dependent systems; BTC / ETH native account signatures do not rely on RSA.

Key counterfactual: Shor long ago provided quantum polynomial-time factoring. #279 is a strengthening of the theoretical form; it does not move RSA from “quantum-theoretically secure” to “quantum-theoretically insecure”, nor does it give classical factoring.

Existing quantum discrete-log algorithm

→
Gap: actual fault-tolerant scale

The required physical qubits, error correction, gate fidelity and runtime must all meet the target.

→
Recover private key / trapdoor, or forge

Targeting secp256k1 signatures, BLS keys and KZG parameters respectively.

Path F · #029 Hecke zero-free theorem → #142 → account signatures / BLS / KZG

Proof dependency exists; attack bridge missing

#029: primitive roots and zero-free regions of Hecke L-functions

Proves that qualifying integers a generate the entire multiplicative group modulo infinitely many primes; its zero-free region theorem is cited by #142.

→
Known link: supports the proof of #142

This step is a mathematical dependency of the paper. What results is deterministic finite-field polynomial factoring, not computing a private key from a public key.

→
Gap: from target key to a solvable problem

An effective attack reduction against secp256k1 / BLS12-381 still has to be established, and the total cost made executable.

→
Conditional endpoint: forged signing / fake votes / fake openings

These bad outcomes are reached only by crossing the same attack thresholds as Paths A–C.

Why it is stuck: Intuition: knowing what generates a group is not the same as being able to write a given element as a particular power of the generator. Cryptographic protocols publish the generator anyway. Even finding primes with smooth p−1 cannot change the already fixed prime-order subgroup r of current curves; conflating the base field p, p−1 and the subgroup order r leads to a wrong attack route.
Gap difficulty: Primary gap D4: a new mathematical algorithm that solves the target DLP or forgery is needed; the difficulty of making it practical afterwards depends on that algorithm.
Original paper: Theorems 1.1, 1.2; the citation relationship with #142.
Where we stand today: Attack-specific thresholds: 0/3; the solid line #029 → #142 does not count as key-attack progress.

Path G · #107 matrix multiplication → finite-field DLP → BLS / KZG / pairing-based ZK

Old NFS route exists; attack gain from #107 not established

#107: faster matrix arithmetic

Main complex-field bound ω≤9/4; in addition, outside finitely many unspecified exceptional characteristics, ω<2.258; and for every fixed field, ω<2.371054886006746. The latter improves on the old bound 2.371177 cited by the paper by about 1.2×10⁻⁴.

→
Known link: linear algebra of finite-field DLP

The old pairing and NFS/TNFS attack routes exist, but there is as yet no effective gain mechanism by which the dense multiplication bound of #107 would help their sparse solving stage.

→
Gap: end-to-end cost actually goes down

One must verify applicability to the target field, sparse-matrix/memory overhead, and the costs of relation collection and individual-target descent, and show that current parameters are attackable.

→
Conditional endpoint: recover keys / trapdoor

If the target-group DLP were executable, BLS keys or the KZG trapdoor could be recovered, in turn threatening consensus signatures, commitments and the corresponding proof systems.

Why it is stuck: NFS-DL solves a sparse system modulo r. Wiedemann/Lanczos commonly cost about w·N² field operations (row weight w). A lower dense exponent still above 2 supplies no gain mechanism for this stage; this is not a lower bound on future sparse or hybrid solvers. Full attacks still require relation collection, descent, memory and finite-size costs. The low-embedding-degree pairing route does not directly apply to secp256k1.
Gap difficulty: No practical gain mechanism is known yet: one would first have to find a new algorithm applicable to sparse solving or to another critical stage, and then estimate the end-to-end cost; progress on the dense exponent cannot be counted as attack progress.
Original paper: main paper Theorem 1.1; all-field companion paper Theorem 1.1; formalization scope.Pairing specification · Four stages of NFS-DL · Sparse solvers
Where we stand today: Existing attack routes remain; #107 shows no new executable attack at current parameters or measured end-to-end speedup.

Path H · #109 integer multiplication → existing cryptanalysis → BTC / ETH / BLS / KZG

Arithmetic link exists; the critical search size is unchanged

#109: integer multiplication below n log n

The model is a fixed multitape Turing machine, κ=2⁻¹⁸². Improves the asymptotic upper bound for n-bit multiplication; no practical crossover point is given.

→
Known link: finite-field arithmetic in every step

Curve group operations, Pollard rho, NFS and so on use integer/finite-field arithmetic; the subroutine connection was always there.

→
Gap: actual savings large enough to cross the security threshold

A somewhat faster single step does not reduce the roughly √r group operations of Pollard rho, nor does it automatically eliminate costs such as relation collection in NFS.

→
Conditional endpoint: actually recover keys / forge

Only if the end-to-end cost drops into a usable range could account theft via forged signatures, BLS forgery or a KZG break result.

Why it is stuck: Theorem 1 states that constants and cutoffs are extremely large; Section 9.8 uses schoolbook multiplication below the cutoff. No cryptographic-size speed gain is established. Substituting n=2048 gives an expression ratio of about 1+3.9×10⁻⁵⁵: a function-value ratio, not an improvement factor or performance measurement. Generic curve-search counts are not reduced.
Gap difficulty: Primary gap D2–D3: implementation and measurement are engineering/algorithmic work; a devastating reduction would still require a substantive improvement in search/factoring algorithms, which is D4.
Original paper: abstract, §1, §9.8; the fixed model and the range of κ.
Where we stand today: The arithmetic subroutine is relevant, but no practical speedup or new executable attack at target security parameters is shown.

Path I · #103 derandomization → assume the target DLP is already solvable in small space → signature failure

No algorithm solving the target; if the gap were filled, the old randomized algorithm would already be a threat

#103: L = RL = BPL

Turns existing polynomial-time, logarithmic-work-space randomized decision programs into deterministic ones.

→
Gap 1: first find a small-space program solving the target

For example, prove that the decision problem for each bit of the private key lies in BPL and that the private key can be reconstructed from the decisions. #103 gives no such cryptanalysis.

→
Gap 2: executable cost at target parameters

Deterministic polynomial time does not guarantee practicality; runtime, the precision parameter and the constants of the specific program must be checked.

→
Conditional endpoint: recover private key and forge signatures

Only if solving the specified target and practicality both hold, and it then passes real verification, does it lead to the failure of account or BLS keys.

Why it is stuck: Key counterfactual: if Gap 1 were filled, BPL⊆P was already known before #103; the original BPL program would already imply deterministic polynomial-time solvability. The novelty of #103 lies in the space bound; the breakthrough in the target algorithm would not come from it. #103 does not prove P=NP, nor does it make all hard problems solvable in small space. The paper’s precision parameter q generally brings 2^O(q) time; one cannot ignore the parameter and then claim general fast exact solving.
Gap difficulty: Primary gap D4: proving that secure-curve DLP / private-key-bit decision lies in this algorithmic class amounts to first finding a breakthrough cryptanalysis.
Original paper: Theorems 1.1, 1.2; the model and time bounds of §1.
Where we stand today: Target-attack thresholds via #103: 0/3. The missing target BPL program is itself the core attack algorithm.

Path J · #002 BSD formula → [missing effective DLP algorithm] → curve signatures

Objects are related; the bridge from global structure to finite-group private keys is missing

#002: BSD under low Selmer corank

Gives the rank, finiteness of Sha and the exact leading-term formula for elliptic curves over the rationals, under a low-corank condition.

→
Gap 1: derive a target private-key algorithm from the formula

d must be extracted from G and Q=dG in the finite field. Knowing the rank, L-values or the global formula does not directly give d.

→
Gap 2: lifting and computation size under control

If lifting to a rational curve is used, one must control the point heights, the representation, the amount of computation and the step back to the finite-field solution.

→
Conditional endpoint: corresponding signatures / commitments fail

Only if the target curve DLP can really be solved at practical cost would ECDSA, Schnorr, BLS or KZG be further threatened.

Why it is stuck: “Curve rank” describes the global structure of the group of rational points; the hard problem behind on-chain public keys is the scalar d in a specified finite group. Lifting a finite-field public key to some rational curve does not, for free, express it as a known combination of small-height generators. The paper has no such reduction, no algorithm at target security parameters and no running cost. The low Selmer corank hypothesis concerns the lifted curve over the rationals; even if the hypothesis is satisfied, an algorithm that effectively lifts G and Q and extracts d is still missing.
Gap difficulty: Primary gap D4: the global arithmetic structure would have to be turned into an effective finite-field DLP solver, with a complexity proof.
Original paper: Theorem 1.1; the low Selmer corank hypothesis and the scope of the formula.
Where we stand today: Attack-specific thresholds: 0/3; a proven structural formula does not mean key solving has completed one hop.

Path K · #030 modularity → [missing effective DLP algorithm] → curve signatures

Structural correspondence holds; it does not output the hidden scalar

#030: modularity of elliptic curves over imaginary quadratic fields

Establishes a correspondence with automorphic representations, matching data such as local parameters and L-functions.

→
Gap 1: make the structural correspondence solve for d

One would need to prove that the correspondence can efficiently extract d from the finite-field target Q=dG, rather than merely yield curve-level information.

→
Gap 2: target curve and algorithm executable

Representation, lifting and computational cost must also be controlled, and validated at secp256k1 / BLS12-381 parameters.

→
Conditional endpoint: corresponding key recovery / forgery

Only if an executable DLP attack is actually obtained would the on-chain damage on the right occur.

Why it is stuck: The Frobenius or local point counts appearing in the paper describe the curve. secp256k1, BLS12-381 and BN254 are CM curves with j=0, and the CM modularity of their natural lifts is a classical case. Knowing #E(𝔽ₚ), the group order or the modularity correspondence is not the same as knowing the discrete logarithm of some public key with respect to the generator. Curve point counts could always be computed with existing algorithms such as Schoof’s; the new modularity conclusion gives no additional key-extraction step.
Gap difficulty: Primary gap D4: deriving a practical DLP algorithm from the modularity correspondence is a major breakthrough not yet provided, not a direct application of the theorem.
Original paper: Theorem 1.1; local parameter / L-function correspondence.
Where we stand today: Attack-specific thresholds: 0/3; there is currently no quantifiable key-recovery increment.
